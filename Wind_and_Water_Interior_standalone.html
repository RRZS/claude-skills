The Origins of
Wind and Water
Three thousand years of paying attention — and what the ancient surveyors of the Yellow River were really looking for.
Fig. 1 · The valley the surveyors read — mountain behind, water before.

The two characters that name our practice — fēng (風) and shuǐ (水) — translate, with disarming plainness, as wind and water. They are the two elements an ancient surveyor could not control and could not ignore: the breath that moves across a valley, the current that decides where a village can take root. To this day, every principle in this book is a refinement of one essential question those surveyors first asked, four thousand years ago: how does life-force move through this place, and how can we live in friendship with it?

Wind & WaterChapter One

A Practice Older Than Books

Long before the discipline acquired a name, Chinese farmers had already learned that some hillsides grew better grain than others, that some bends in a river attracted settlements while others bred plague. The practice we now call Feng Shui began as agricultural common sense — the patient observation of how landscape, climate, and orientation conspired to bless or curse a piece of ground.

By the time of the Han Dynasty, that common sense had been refined into a literate discipline. The surveyor — the kanyu jia, the one who reads “heaven and earth” — carried a magnetic compass, a set of bronze rulers, and a thick book of correspondences. He would walk a parcel of land for days before declaring it auspicious. He was not a mystic. He was a kind of meteorologist of the spirit.

Two Great Schools

From the classical period emerged two main approaches that still organize the field today. The Form School, sometimes called Luan Tou, reads the visible landscape: the curve of a mountain behind a house, the shape of a road approaching the door, the way water gathers or rushes past. Its working metaphor is the green dragon to the left and the white tiger to the right of any seated position — the embracing arms of the land.

Fig. 2 · The luopan — the surveyor’s compass of concentric rings.

The Compass School, or Li Qi, works with the unseen: the eight directions, the trigrams of the I Ching, the cycles of time. Its principal instrument is the luopan, a black-lacquered compass crowded with concentric rings of Chinese characters. Practitioners of compass Feng Shui calculate where to place a door the way an astronomer calculates an eclipse.

This book teaches both. The Form School helps you choose a house. The Compass School helps you live in it.

“The mountain holds you. The water finds you. Between the two, you build a life.”

Why It Survived

Feng Shui has outlasted empires, weathered the suspicion of communists and the gentle ridicule of moderns, and crossed every ocean. It survived because, at root, it is not a system of superstition. It is a system of attention. To practice Feng Shui is to notice things most people do not notice: the imperceptible draft under a badly-hung door, the slight oppression of a beam over the bed, the way a cluttered hallway slows you down before you have even put on your shoes. The practice rewards observation. Observation rewards the practitioner. That is the whole of it.

Qi — The Breath
of the World
On the substance that is everywhere and never quite anywhere — and how to recognize its movement through a room.
Fig. 3 · Qi enters at the door and drifts in soft, unhurried curves.

If the practice of Feng Shui has a single subject of study, it is qi. Qi is the energy that animates everything from a falling leaf to a financial decision. It is not mystical in the sense of being remote; it is mystical in the sense of being too close — too constant — for casual notice. The whole craft of Feng Shui can be described as the cultivation of the senses required to feel qi at work and the discipline required to invite it into the rooms where you live.

Wind & WaterChapter Two

What Qi Is, and What It Isn’t

Qi is not a fluid. It is not a substance that can be poured. It is closer in nature to a tendency — the inclination of a place toward life or against it. A garden in full bloom has lively qi. A hospital corridor at three in the morning has thinned qi. A landlord’s storage unit, untouched for seven years, has stagnant qi. None of these descriptions requires belief. They require only the willingness to notice what is already there.

Three States of Qi

The classical literature distinguishes three principal modes in which qi can be found.

  • Sheng qi — the rising breath. Vital, generative, and abundant. You feel it in a room that has just been cleaned, in a kitchen full of cooking, in a freshly weeded garden.
  • Sha qi — the cutting breath. Hostile, sharp, often the result of geometry. Long straight corridors, sharp corners pointed at the bed, the edge of a neighbor’s roof aimed at your front door.
  • Si qi — the stagnant breath. Heavy, listless, and slow. Found in cluttered basements, behind furniture that has not been moved in a decade, beneath the bed of someone who is not sleeping well.

The work of the practitioner — your work, beginning today — is to encourage sheng qi, deflect sha qi, and disperse si qi. Almost every cure in the second half of this book is a refinement of one of these three movements.

A useful experiment. Walk into your own home with your shoes still on, as a guest would. Note the first three things your eye lands on, and the first sensation in your chest. That sensation is your honest reading of the qi.

How Qi Moves

Qi enters a building primarily through the front door — which is why the door receives a chapter of its own (see Chapter Nine). From the door, qi behaves rather like a slow gas: it prefers to drift in soft curves, to linger where it is welcome, to escape where the geometry presses it out. Long unbroken hallways accelerate qi until it has nothing left to give. Cluttered corners trap it. Mirrors redirect it. A round table at the center of a room collects it.

What you are arranging, when you arrange furniture, is not really furniture. It is the path the breath of the world will take through your rooms tomorrow morning.

The Five Elements
& their Cycles
Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water — and the two great cycles that govern how they nourish or consume one another.
Fig. 4 · Green pentagon — generation. Red star — control.

The Chinese conception of an element is not the chemical one. The Five Elements are not substances; they are phases — five recognizable modes that energy passes through as it moves around the world. Wood is the phase of beginning. Fire is the phase of full expression. Earth is the phase of settling. Metal is the phase of refinement. Water is the phase of return.

The Productive Cycle

The first cycle is one of mutual nourishment. Water feeds Wood, Wood feeds Fire, Fire produces Earth (as ash), Earth bears Metal, Metal condenses Water. When you arrange a room according to the productive cycle, the energies pass smoothly from one to the next, like an exhale: a wooden bowl on an earthen table beside a candle.

The Destructive Cycle

The second cycle is one of mutual restraint. Wood breaks Earth, Earth dams Water, Water quenches Fire, Fire melts Metal, Metal cuts Wood. The destructive cycle is not a curse; it is a discipline. We use it deliberately to soften an element that is overpowering a room — a fish tank (water) placed thoughtfully near a south-facing fireplace (fire) to keep the room from running hot.

Mnemonic · the two cycles
Generation feeds. Control restrains.
Both cycles are at work in every room. The practitioner’s skill is recognizing which one a given space is calling for.

The Bagua —
A Map of Eight
Eight directions, eight life domains, and the simple octagonal grid you can lay over the floor plan of any room.

If the Five Elements are the grammar of Feng Shui, the Bagua is its alphabet. Ba means eight; gua means trigram. The Bagua is an octagonal arrangement of the eight foundational trigrams of the I Ching, each of which corresponds to a direction, an element, a life area, a family member, a color, and a season. Laid over the floor plan of a room or a house, it becomes the most useful diagnostic tool in the practice.

Calculating Your Kua Number

Your Kua is a single digit between 1 and 9 — derived from your year of birth and your gender — that points you toward four lucky directions and away from four unlucky ones.

Worksheet · Five steps
Find your Kua number
  1. Write down the year of your birth in lunar terms. (If born in January or early February, see the lunar calendar appendix; you may need to use the previous year.)
  2. Take the last two digits of that year. Add them together.
  3. If the result is two digits, reduce again to a single digit. Example: 1974 → 7+4 = 11 → 1+1 = 2.
  4. If male: subtract that number from 10. (Born in 2000 or later: subtract from 9.)
  5. If female: add 5 to that number. Reduce again if needed. (Born in 2000 or later: add 6.)

There is one small exception. The number 5 has no Kua of its own — its energy is reassigned. If your calculation lands on 5, your true Kua is 2 if male and 8 if female.

Result
Kua 1, 3, 4, 9 → East Group
Kua 2, 6, 7, 8 → West Group
Your group determines which four directions feed you and which four drain you. The full table follows in Chapter 8.

The Bedroom
& the Practice of Rest
The single most important room in any Feng Shui practice — because it is the room in which you are least defended.
Fig. 5 · The commanding position — door in view, never in line.

In every Feng Shui survey I have ever conducted, the bedroom has been the room that, once corrected, produced the most disproportionate change in the life of the person living in it. We spend a third of our lives there. We are unconscious. The room arranges us as much as we arrange it.

Wind & WaterChapter Twelve

The Commanding Position

The single rule that, if you remember nothing else from this chapter, will serve you for a lifetime: place your bed in the commanding position. This means you should be able to see the bedroom door from your bed, without being directly in line with it. Diagonally opposite the door is usually ideal. The wall behind the headboard should be solid — not a window. The bed should not float in the middle of the room.

The deeper logic is biological. A body that cannot see the entry to a room remains, all night, on a low simmer of vigilance. Move the bed, and the simmer ends.

What to Remove

  • Mirrors facing the bed. Whatever else you keep, remove these tonight. Mirrors agitate the qi of a sleeping body. If immovable, cover at night.
  • Active electronics. Televisions, exercise equipment, work laptops. The bedroom is for two activities; nothing on this list is one of them.
  • Storage under the bed. Particularly anything emotionally charged: old letters, photographs, taxes. The body absorbs what is below it.
  • Sharp corners aimed at the bed. Bookcases, desks, wardrobe edges. Soften with a plant or reposition.

What to Add

A bedroom that has been emptied of distractions should not be left austere; austerity has its own kind of sha qi. Replace what you removed with objects of beauty and stillness: a single artwork above the bed, a pair of matching bedside lamps (the pairing matters — it nourishes partnership), a soft wool rug under bare feet, curtains heavy enough to muffle the morning.

A bed is not a piece of furniture. It is a small country in which you are sovereign for eight hours every night. Govern it well.

Decluttering as
a Ritual Practice
Why the simple act of removing what you no longer need is the most powerful Feng Shui intervention available to you.
Fig. 6 · Every object meets one of three baskets.

Most Feng Shui consultations end the way they begin: with the recommendation to throw things away. Not because the practice is austere, but because nothing else moves stagnant qi as completely as the honest, ruthless, and slightly tearful work of letting go.

The classical Chinese term for clutter is the same as the term for siltation — the slow accumulation of unmoving matter at the bottom of a river that, if left, will eventually divert the river entirely. A home accumulates silt in exactly the same way. The closet that never closes properly. The mail tray that has not been emptied since spring. The drawer in the kitchen.

The work begins with a single ritual, performed in any room of your choice. Find one drawer. Remove every item. Place each item in one of three piles: keep, release, uncertain. The third pile is the practice; the first two are easy. Sit with the uncertain pile for at least one minute per object, and ask: does this serve the person I am now, or the person I was?

Most objects, given that question, will answer for themselves. The few that remain genuinely uncertain belong, for the moment, in storage. Return to them in one month. They will, almost without exception, have answered by then.

Glossary of Terms

A working vocabulary for the practitioner. Pinyin transliteration in italic; Chinese characters where useful.

Bagua八卦
The octagonal map of the eight trigrams; the principal diagnostic tool of Feng Shui, applied to homes, rooms, and individual surfaces.
Chi · Qi氣
The vital life-force that animates and moves through all phenomena; the principal subject of Feng Shui practice.
Form School巒頭
The classical tradition that reads the visible landscape — mountains, water, roads — to assess auspiciousness.
Kua Number卦數
A single digit derived from year of birth and gender, used to determine personal auspicious directions.
Luopan羅盤
The traditional Feng Shui compass, used in the Compass School tradition to take precise directional readings.
Sha Qi煞氣
Hostile or cutting energy, often produced by sharp angles, long corridors, or arrow-like geometries pointed at a dwelling.
Sheng Qi生氣
Vital, rising, generative energy; the energy a well-arranged room actively cultivates.
Si Qi死氣
Stagnant, lifeless energy; the energy of clutter, dust, and abandonment. Dispersed by movement, light, and air.
Yin · Yang陰 · 陽
The two complementary polarities — receptive and active, dark and light, still and moving — whose balance underlies all Feng Shui adjustment.

About the Author

Rayan R. Sleem

Rayan R. Sleem is a fourth-generation Feng Shui practitioner trained in both the Form and Compass schools at the Mastery Academy of Chinese Metaphysics. Sleem has consulted on over two thousand homes and businesses across three continents, from a single-room studio in Brooklyn to the corporate headquarters of a Fortune 500 company in Singapore.

This work blends the strict classical lineage of that training with a more contemporary sensitivity to the soulful, lived-in nature of modern homes. Sleem lectures regularly at the Mastery Academy, contributes to the Journal of Eastern Geomancy, and keeps a small consulting practice from a home in the foothills outside Kyoto.

Wind & Water is Sleem’s first full-length book.
