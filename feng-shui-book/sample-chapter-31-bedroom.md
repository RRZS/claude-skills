# Chapter 31 — The Bedroom: Rest, Love, and Restoration

*Part VII · Room by Room Mastery*

> *Of all the rooms you will ever tend, one holds you while you cannot tend yourself. Tend it first.*

### The room that works while you sleep

Every other room in your home, you meet awake. You walk into the kitchen and you act upon it; you cross the living room and you arrange it to your liking. The traffic of influence runs outward, from you to the room.

In the bedroom, the traffic runs the other way.

For seven or eight hours of every night you lie still, unconscious, your guard entirely down — and the room acts upon you. You are never more Yin than in sleep, never more open, never less defended. Whatever the bedroom holds — the condition of its Qi, its order or its disorder, the direction it faces, the images hung upon its walls — presses, gently and without pause, into a body that has stopped defending itself.

You will spend roughly a third of your life in this one room. The tradition's counsel is therefore blunt: of all the work in this book, begin here. And the bedroom repays that attention faster than any other room in the home. Set a living room right and the household slowly warms. Set a bedroom right and people often report, within a week or two, that they are simply sleeping again — and that the rest of their life has steadied because its foundation has.

### The command position

The single most important thing you will do in this room — more important than its color, more important than its compass direction, more important than any cure you might add — is to place the bed well. A bed is placed well when it sits in the **command position**.

The command position is exact, and worth stating precisely. From your pillow, you should be able to see the door of the room — yet the bed should not lie directly in line with it. The strong placement is diagonal: the headboard against a solid wall, the bed set toward the corner farthest from the door, the doorway in clear view across the room.

This is not arbitrary, and it is older than Feng Shui itself. It is the body's most ancient instinct: to rest with the way in held within sight, and with something solid at one's back. A sleeper who can see the door surrenders to sleep at a depth a sleeper who cannot will never reach — because some watchful, wordless part of the mind, unable to confirm that it is safe, keeps its small night-watch until dawn. Place the bed in command, and you relieve that sentry of its duty.

One placement must be refused outright. Never set the bed in a straight line with the door, feet pointing out through it. The tradition names this starkly — the **coffin position** — because the dead are carried out feet-first. Set the grim name aside, and a plain fact remains: a bed in the doorway's line receives the full rushing current of Qi that enters there — the cutting breath we met in Chapter 5 — drawn along the sleeping body all night long.

And the grounded truth, because most bedrooms are small and stubborn: you may not be able to achieve the ideal. If the only wall that will take a headboard places you in the door's line, shift the bed even a little to one side, so your body does not lie in the current. Set a footboard, a low chest, or a bench at the foot of the bed to break the line. Reach for the principle first; accept the nearest honest compromise second.

### A mountain behind, clear sky above

A bed needs a *mountain* behind it. In Chapter 23 we met the Form School's four celestial animals — and the first of them, the Black Tortoise, is the high, solid ground at one's back. The headboard is the bedroom's tortoise. It is the support behind the sleeper, and it should be worthy of the name.

Choose a headboard that is solid and substantial, firmly fixed to the bed, set against a solid wall. A headboard of thin bars or open lattice gives weaker support than a full panel of wood or upholstered board. No headboard at all leaves the sleeper's crown exposed and unbacked. Worst of all is the bed whose head stands against a window: a wall of glass is no mountain — it offers no support, it leaks Qi, and it lets a draught cross the very part of the body that most needs stillness. If your bed must stand at a window, hang a heavy curtain or a solid blind, and choose the most substantial headboard you can.

Then look up. What hangs above a sleeping body bears down upon it. An exposed beam crossing the bed presses a line of heavy, compressing Qi onto whatever lies beneath it — and the tradition is specific: a beam over the head is linked to headaches and crowded, pressured thoughts; a beam crossing the bed between two sleepers, to a couple who slowly divide. The cures, in order of preference: move the bed clear of the beam; if you cannot, soften the beam — drape fabric across it, paint it to recede into the ceiling, or hang from it two bamboo flutes (Chapter 36). A sloping ceiling presses in the same way; sleep beneath its higher side. Heavy shelves, cabinets, and glass-framed pictures above the headboard belong somewhere else. As far as you are able, the space above your sleep should be open, level, and clear — clear sky over the mountain.

### Room for two

A bed should be approachable from both sides — and this is true even of the bed of someone who sleeps alone.

A bed shoved hard against a wall along one of its long sides forces one sleeper to climb over the other to come and go. The body learns, night after night, the lesson the room is teaching: there is room here for one, and the second person is an obstacle. Pull the bed away from the wall. Give it open floor on both long sides.

Then set, on each side, a nightstand — two of them, a matched pair — and on each a lamp. This symmetry is not mere tidiness. Two equal places, two equal lights, tell the room, and the partnership it shelters, that two people belong here on equal ground.

And for the single person who hopes one day to welcome a partner: a bed wedged into a corner, with one side against the wall and a single table, is a room arranged around someone's absence. Do not wait for the person to arrive before you make the place. Draw the bed into the open. Set the second nightstand and the second lamp now. The room is an instruction, and the life you are inviting will read it.

> A young couple, married less than a year, called a practitioner to a bedroom they had begun to dread. They did not put it that way. They said only that they were tired, that they had started to quarrel over nothing, and that the wife in particular slept badly and woke unrested.
>
> The room was small, and they had done what small rooms tempt everyone to do: pushed the bed into the far corner, hard against two walls, to win back a little floor. It was the sensible thing. It was also, the practitioner saw at once, the whole of the trouble.
>
> The wife slept on the inside, against the wall. To come to bed she climbed over her husband; to rise in the night she climbed over him again. Her side held no table, no lamp, no ground of her own — there was no room for one. And from her pillow, set deep in the corner, she could see neither the door nor the window: only wall, close on two sides, the way a person is held in a place they cannot leave.
>
> "You have given your marriage a single doorway," the practitioner said, "and asked two people to share it. One of you must always be climbing. The body does not forget a lesson the room repeats every night."
>
> He did a plain thing. He drew the bed out of the corner until it stood clear of both walls, its headboard alone against the solid wall, the door in view across the room. And he found space — there is almost always space, once a room is asked the right question — for two small tables and two lamps, one to each side. The floor they had lost was the floor they had been quarreling to keep.
>
> The wife wrote some weeks later. She was sleeping through the night, she said. She had not known that a bedroom could be the kind of thing that was wrong — or the kind of thing that could be set right.

### Turning the bed toward your good direction

Once the bed sits in command, a further refinement is open to you — one that draws on the personal compass you calculated in Part Four. Where the room allows it, orient the head of the bed — the wall the headboard stands against — toward one of the sleeper's four auspicious directions (Chapter 14). For deep rest and recovery from illness, choose *Tian Yi*, the health direction, the "heavenly doctor." For a couple seeking warmth and a lasting bond, choose *Yan Nian*, the direction of relationships.

Hold the order of priority firmly, because here a reader can go astray. **Command position comes first; direction comes second.** A bed that points to your finest compass direction but leaves you startled, exposed, and unable to see the door is a bed that faces the wrong way. Secure safety and support first. Then, within the placements that keep them, turn toward your best direction.

When two partners belong to different groups — one of the East, one of the West (Chapter 15) — a single headboard wall cannot serve both. Let the bed serve the partner in greater need: the one recovering, the one whose sleep is poorest. Or accept a direction that wrongs neither badly. Never let the calculation override the harmony of the room; the aim is not a perfect compass reading but two people who both, in fact, rest.

### What to carry out of the bedroom

A bedroom heals as much by what it lacks as by what it holds. Four things, gently but firmly, do not belong in the room of rest.

**Mirrors that face the bed.** A mirror is an active object — it moves Qi, it doubles it, it casts it back across the room all night against a body that is trying to fall still. The tradition holds, further, that a mirror reflecting a couple's bed invites a third presence into the marriage. The usual culprit is the mirrored wardrobe door. Curtain it, cover it at night, or re-hang the mirror where it cannot see the bed.

**The bright, humming world of screens.** The television, the computer, the phone charging by the pillow, the exercise machine in the corner — each carries restless Yang energy and the business of the working, outer world into the one room you keep for rest. A bedroom asked to be also an office and also a gym cannot be wholly a bedroom. Remove them where you can; at the very least, cover the screen at night and banish the phone from the bedside.

**The hoard beneath the bed.** Qi should circulate freely around the sleeping body, above it and below. Whatever is stored under the bed sits beneath you all night — and stale storage breeds the dying breath, the *Si Qi* of Chapter 5, which then rises into your sleep. Worst are shoes that have walked the outer world, old papers, anything to do with work, anything sharp, and anything kept from a former relationship. If a small home truly leaves you no other storage, keep only soft, sleep-related things there — spare bedding, linens — and nothing else.

**Water.** Keep water out of the place of sleep: no fountains, no fish tanks, and not even pictures whose true subject is water. The bedroom is a Yin room of stillness, and water is movement; the tradition links water in the bedroom to seepage and loss. A glass at the bedside is no matter — a water *feature* is. (A related, gentler caution: many teachers also keep large, vigorous plants out of the bedroom, holding that their striving Wood energy is too active for a room of rest. One small plant will not trouble a room — but the bedroom is not the place for a thriving indoor garden.)

### Dressing the room for rest

With the bed placed and the room unburdened, attend to its senses.

**Color.** Reach for soft, low, restful tones. The classical guidance offers a palette of "skin tones" — the whole quiet range from palest cream through sand and clay to deep cocoa-brown — earthy, enveloping, neither cold nor stimulating. Within that calm, you may draw in a gentle wash of a color from your own favorable element (Chapter 9) to personalize the room. Use sparingly the large fields of vivid red and intense orange: these are the colors of Fire (Chapter 7) — of passion, yes, but also of restlessness — and a bedroom drenched in them is a bedroom that will not let the mind sit down.

**Light.** Make the light layered and dimmable. The single hard ceiling light is the enemy of evening. Give the room bedside lamps and, if you can, a dimmer; welcome the warm, low flame of candlelight. The room should be able to soften as the night itself softens.

**Texture and the bed itself.** The bed is the heart of the room; furnish it as such. Choose, where means allow, a solid wooden frame over a metal one, a good mattress, and sheets of natural fiber — cotton or linen — that breathe with the body. Natural materials hold a calmer, more nourishing Qi than synthetics.

**Air.** Keep the air fresh: open the windows by day to clear away the night's stale breath. But do not let a draught run directly across the bed — fresh air is a blessing, a steady cold current over the sleeper is not.

### The room of the partnership

The master bedroom belongs, in its energy, to the couple — and it should be allowed to. Family photographs, the children's drawings, the portraits of parents and friends: give these the living room and the hall. A master bedroom crowded with the whole family's faces is a marriage that has kept no room closed to the world. Keep this one room for the partnership it shelters.

Where pairs will serve, use pairs — two lamps, two nightstands, objects and images in twos rather than ones and threes (the fuller language of auspicious symbols waits in Chapter 39). And hang upon the walls only what you wish to live inside: art in the bedroom should depict the life you mean to invite — tenderness, calm, two of a thing, a settled horizon. Refuse the sorrowful solitary figure, the storm-tossed sea, the bare winter branch. The walls of the room that works upon you while you sleep should never be quietly arguing against you.

If a former love still hangs framed on the wall, or sleeps in a box beneath the bed, then it is still, in some measure, in the marriage. Carry it out — and, when you do, consider the space clearing of Chapter 41, so that the room is not merely emptied but renewed.

Finally, the room's place in the home. The master bedroom rests best in a quiet, settled, more Yin quarter of the house — toward the rear, away from the street's noise and the busy traffic of the front door, not perched above a garage, not pressed wall-to-wall against the kitchen's Fire. A bedroom should be a destination, never a thoroughfare.

You will not make this room perfect; few rooms permit it. But the bedroom rewards even a little care more visibly, and more swiftly, than any other room in the home — because it has you for a third of your life, undefended and willing to be changed. Tend it first. Then lie down, and let the room do the rest.

> ### Practice — The View from the Pillow
>
> Tonight, before you sleep, survey your bedroom from the one vantage point that matters: your own pillow.
>
> 1. **Lie down** in your usual place, head on the pillow, and simply look. Can you see the door of the room? Are your feet pointed straight out through it? (If they are, the command position is asking you to move the bed.)
> 2. **Reach behind and above.** Is there a solid headboard, and a solid wall behind it — or a window, or nothing at all? Look up: is anything overhead — a beam, a heavy shelf, a low-sloping ceiling?
> 3. **Sit up and turn.** Does any mirror return your reflection to the bed? Is a screen watching the room? Now look beneath the bed, and name honestly what is stored there.
> 4. **Stand in the doorway** and look back at the bed as the Qi would meet it. Is the bed reachable from both sides? Are there two of the small things — two tables, two lamps?
> 5. **Choose one change** — the single most important one you can make this week — and write it down. Then, this week, make it.
>
> Return to the pillow once the change is done, and look again. A bedroom is set right not all at once, but one honest look at a time.
